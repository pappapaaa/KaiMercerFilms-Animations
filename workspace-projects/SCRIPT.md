# The Tower That Chases Shade

Original science short. English narration, 9:16, 30 fps, approximately 60 seconds. No on-screen captions. Cartoon illustrative motion is exaggerated; the narration explicitly states that real movement is too small to notice. Original characters: a curious tourist in a red cap and a Parisian guide in a blue jacket.

## Verified facts

- The iron structure expands as it warms and contracts as it cools. Sunlight heating one face more than the other faces causes a slight lean away from the sun.
- The summit can trace a roughly circular curve approximately 15 cm in diameter during a clear day. **This is sideways movement, not a claim of 15 cm vertical growth.**
- These small natural movements do not compromise the structure and are imperceptible to visitors.
- Primary source: Eiffel Tower operator, https://www.toureiffel.paris/en/news/history-and-culture/why-does-eiffel-tower-change-size (accessed 2026-11-02).
- Atomic illustration is schematic: increased vibration and slightly greater average separation in heated metal. Atoms are not shown inflating. No quantitative microscopic scale is implied.

## Narration

The Eiffel Tower leans away from the sun. And nobody is pushing it.

Imagine visiting Paris on a blazing summer afternoon. You take a picture of the tower. It looks completely still. But that enormous iron structure is quietly changing shape.

First, the metal warms up. Then the tower grows a little taller. And here comes the strange part. The side facing the sun heats up more than the side in the shade. So one side stretches more than the other.

The result? The top bends away from the sunlight. The tower is basically trying to find some shade. Very slowly. And very unsuccessfully.

You see, iron isn't perfectly still inside. Its atoms vibrate. Heat them up, and their average spacing increases. Tiny changes add up across an entire tower.

Think of two friends walking arm in arm. If one takes slightly longer steps, the pair starts turning. Uneven expansion gives the tower a similar little bend.

As the sun moves across the sky, the summit can trace a curve roughly fifteen centimeters across. Too small for you to notice. Completely normal for the tower.

When the iron cools, it contracts again. So that famous Paris landmark spends its sunny days doing the world's slowest sun dodge.
